Most students know the feeling: sometimes it seems to take forever to understand something new, and sometimes it just clicks. That is why, especially in science and mathematics programs, lectures are accompanied by exercise classes in which the material is practiced, applied, verified or proved. On top of that, there are tutorials, study groups, office hours with teaching staff and much more.

Still, for some students these offers are not enough, or they are unable to take advantage of them. In mathematics, gaps in understanding can quickly become serious, because the content of typical mathematics lectures builds on itself. Once you fall behind, it is often hard to follow what comes next.

There are many ways to close such gaps, and working through exercises is one of them. But that is not always easy. This is why my exercise and study books contain a wide range of problems designed to support students as they learn. [Übungsbuch Analysis I](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-662-64569-7) and [Übungsbuch Analysis II](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-662-65832-1) even include several practice exams with model solutions, so students can test themselves while preparing for their exams.

When working on an exercise, the actual learning process begins with understanding the problem itself:

> _What am I supposed to do? What is being asked of me?_

The student looks up the relevant terms, definitions and notation, and then starts working on the problem, developing a strategy and putting it into practice. It is perfectly normal that not every approach works, and sometimes a new strategy is needed. But even a second or third attempt does not always lead to success.

> _I understand the problem, but I have no idea how to approach it._

At this point there are usually two options: give up, or look for a model solution. And often the realization is:

> _With the right hint, I could have done this myself._

That is why my exercise and study books contain not only problems and model solutions, but also a hint for every single exercise. The hints point only to the key ideas and strategies that matter for the problem, so that students can then solve it on their own. After all, sometimes all you need is a little nudge.
